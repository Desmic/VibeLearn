## Highest-priority findings

### 1. The opening behaves like six illustrated instruction cards

**Observed:** a largely repeated diorama, a six-part progress strip, large heading, explanatory paragraph, Pip line, imperative instruction, world action label, duplicate bottom action, and navigation controls. The first beacon changes the world, and subsequent states do change; this is not a static image. Nevertheless, the reading/Continue rhythm dominates.

On desktop the world occupies a central band with extensive empty dark space above and explanatory space below. On phone the islands are cropped tightly; the Forge is outside the initial view. Neither view gives a strong readable sense of a valley of seven connected communities.

**Newcomer consequence:** the player learns the setting from assertions in captions. The screen has not yet earned the concepts “valley,” “courier,” “keeper,” “Forge,” or “ember.” “Pip is almost home” helps, but the geography, destination, and character relationship remain weak without reading.

**Contract:** GAME-OPENING-PROGRESSION explicitly requires who/where/need/player-role comprehension and rejects six pages of explanation over scenery. GAME-AS-COURSE requires attention and attachment before cognition.

Evidence: [desktop opening](playtest-20260915/opening-desktop.png), [phone opening replay](playtest-20260915/opening-phone.png).

### 2. The important cause and consequence leave the phone frame

**Observed:** at the first mission's default view, the avatar and foreground island dominate. The Forge is largely offscreen. The screen says “Look what happened / Two gears,” but the duplicate gears and consumed resource cannot be inspected in that default phone frame. The consequence is visible more clearly in the desktop background.

The opening's lightning appears over the home area; without captions it can read as a strike on the building rather than a cut in a returning message. “Last ember” and “Waiting for help” are labels on small/distant elements, not a strongly staged relationship between fuel, production, and a neighbor who needs it.

**Newcomer consequence:** clicking produces a verdict to read rather than a cause to understand. Removing the prose would remove much of the learning feedback. Free camera is valuable but should not require a novice to hunt for the result of their own first action.

Evidence: [duplicate at 390px](playtest-20260915/duplicate-phone.png), [360px](playtest-20260915/duplicate-360.png), [430px](playtest-20260915/duplicate-430.png), [desktop comparison](playtest-20260915/duplicate-desktop.png).

### 3. HUD labels do not consistently explain their relationship to the world

**Observed:** “Echo Forge ↗” appears at the left edge of the phone while the actual Forge is beyond the right side of the composition. Labels for destinations, actions, and unavailable actions share pill-shaped treatment. The initial action marker is near Pip, and can read as a character label. The teal player avatar is introduced only at mission entry, with a textual “You are the Signal Keeper.” Pip is a different, much larger robot.

Terminology also changes between seal, ticket, order, promise, journal, order book, Forge and workshop. These are understandable to an informed reviewer but add unnecessary object/function translation for a new player. “Inspect order book” and “Read the order book” are separate actions without a clear advance explanation of why.

**Required revision:** consistent object names; separate visual treatment for object identity, current action, and offscreen direction; anchor labels to an unmistakable object with a line or focus response. Introduce the player visually when Pip asks for help. Keep only the current meaningful marker prominent.

Evidence: [Signal 1 phone](playtest-20260915/signal1-phone.png), [first choice](playtest-20260915/first-choice-phone.png).

### 4. Text obstructs the game and repeats itself

**Observed:** Signal 1 stacks a persistent situation/objective panel, coach panel, bottom ticket/dialogue/actions panel, spatial labels, camera toolbar, and movement stick. The same situation is repeated in the top panel and Pip's bottom panel. At 360px even the objective panel develops its own scrollbar. The desktop action dock also shows horizontal scrolling.

At Signal 6 the viewport contains objective, storm/results panel, route slots, block explanation, horizontally scrolling toolbelt, action button, and a success card laid over the controls. All may technically fit or scroll while still obscuring the world.

**Required revision:** one short current objective, one contextual action, and brief optional subtitles. Expandable details should open deliberately and yield space when play resumes. Keep the explanatory journal accessible; preserve keyboard/screen-reader semantics. Hiding necessary explanation is not the fix—stage the event so less explanation is necessary.

Evidence: [first choice](playtest-20260915/first-choice-phone.png), [Signal 6](playtest-20260915/signal6-phone.png).

### 5. Reproduced defect: camera/movement buttons remain disabled after commands

Reproduction on this revision:

1. Open/resume Signal 1, reload; Recenter, Zoom, Help and movement buttons are enabled.
2. Inspect Echo Forge.
3. Wait for “Saved” and the Ticket objective.
4. Camera and movement **buttons** remain disabled. Ordinary task buttons continue working.
5. Reload: camera/movement buttons become enabled again.

The disabled state persisted through several missions sharing the world. Direct world dragging still worked when tested, so this is not a claim that all navigation/input stopped. The transfer world's newly created buttons worked.

Likely code path to investigate: `web/app.js:261` disables all document buttons; `:300` reenables only buttons currently attached to the document. The persistent runtime stage can be detached/reparented during render (`web/game-runtime.js:94`, with asynchronous migration). Its buttons may miss reenabling. This is a source-supported hypothesis, not a patched/verified root cause.

Add a regression asserting button usability **after** a real persisted command and after reuse of the same world. The current player-controls test checks camera position preservation after inspection, but does not assert the camera buttons are enabled at that point.

Evidence: [accessibility state after inspection](playtest-20260915/controls-after-inspect.txt).

### 6. Signal 3 contradicts its own changed-payload explanation

Observed text: “Current retry: three large gears,” while the Current ticket panel still says “one bridge gear.” The rendered ticket does not embody the mismatch the player must diagnose.

`web/rescue-game.js:81` renders `s.payload || 'one bridge gear'`. The scenario tracks a changed-request condition and provides prose, but the visible ticket's default remains the original generic payload. Show saved and requested contents side by side as distinct objects with correct quantities. Preserve the existing assessment meaning.

### 7. Later levels change the prose more than the experience

Signals 2–5 mostly reuse the same composition and scrollable console. The lesson variations are substantive, but restart, changed parcel, expired memory, and unavailable record are largely introduced as text rather than new visible situations.

Repeated success copy—“Route stable / Pip can move again”—does not explain the particular accomplishment. Holding a conflicting order is a different success from producing a gear. The world and Pip should respond to that difference.

Signal 6 gives the player useful construction agency, but the slots float over the avatar and the storm results remain a panel. It needs a visually traceable route: which step ran, what it inspected, where it stopped, and why the physical outcome differs.

### 8. Transfer is too abrupt and can be solved by repeating the prior recipe

Signal 7 changes to an export-service world and abruptly introduces job ID, attempts, retention in seconds, and shipping a policy. The controls still say ticket/parcel. The same four-block sequence that just cleared Signal 6 cleared Signal 7; no remapping or explanation was required from this player.

This supports a limited claim that the policy behaves correctly on the selected cases. It does not establish that a novice understands export workers, can implement this in a real system, or can transfer independently after a delay. The repair lab is a useful next artifact, but it requires outside coding work and was not completed here.

The step should first establish what the new system does through a concrete file request and consequence, then gradually connect old and new names. Genuine transfer should require a fresh discriminating decision, not only the remembered sequence.

Evidence: [Signal 7 phone](playtest-20260915/signal7-phone.png), [completed map](playtest-20260915/completed-map-phone.png).

## Strengths worth preserving

- One-order versus duplicate-order is a useful, concrete concept for this story.
- The safe first beacon interaction and Pip reaching home give the chapter usable emotional anchors.
- Back, Skip, scene replay, and optional opening replay support deliberate pacing.
- Mistakes are recoverable. The sequence broadens from identity to meaning, time, uncertainty, composition and transfer.
- The actual run reached seven clears; final route recovery survived reload; opening replay preserved completed progress.
- XP was hidden throughout the playthrough. The game remained operable without reward pressure.
- The learning/evidence boundaries and reusable world-spec architecture should remain intact. None of these engineering strengths earns automatic engagement or comprehension credit.

## Recommended next increment: opening through first visible recovery

Keep the same episode and learning semantics. Refine one complete first-minute/first-mission sequence before widening the art pass or adding content.

| Beat | What the player should see/do | Minimal supporting language |
|---|---|---|
| Home and role | Follow Pip carrying a parcel toward a visibly occupied, warm home; Pip notices the player's unlit beacon. | “That's my home. Could you light the way?” |
| Safe first success | Touch the actual beacon; the path lights progressively; Pip looks back and acknowledges the player. | “Thanks!” |
| Interruption | Pip reaches the crossing; its working gear visibly cracks and the crossing sags. Frame the broken mechanism clearly. | “Oh. We need one new gear.” |
| One job | Pip stamps a distinctive ticket depicting one gear. Track it to the Forge; show one ember powering one gear. | “This ticket is for one gear.” |
| Lost answer | Track a separate reply on its way back; lightning cuts that signal; the home and bridge remain spatially distinct. | “No reply… did it work?” |
| Player decision | Pip asks the Keeper for help; focus the Forge. Let inspection reveal the completed gear and its matching ticket. | “Let's check before ordering again.” |
| Recovery/payoff | Reuse the ticket; deliver that same gear, repair the bridge, let Pip cross home; show a neighbor still able to receive the spare ember. | “One gear. You got me home.” |

These lines are an illustrative staging treatment, not a new assessment/content snapshot. Preserve player-versus-character knowledge: the audience may have seen the Forge act while Pip still lacks confirmation.

### Observable acceptance for that increment

- A fresh player can point to Pip, home, the bridge, Forge, and their own role without a glossary.
- With the large exposition panels absent, the visible sequence still communicates the need, the lost reply, and the duplicate risk; accessible short descriptions remain available.
- On 360/390/430px, the active object and the consequence remain visible beside the necessary controls. No first-action result requires hunting offscreen.
- The duplicate path visibly shows a second gear and loss of the spare ember; the safe path visibly repairs the crossing and gets Pip home.
- The current-object marker and its tap target agree. Camera/movement buttons stay usable after persisted actions.
- Back/skip/replay, local and hosted isolation, server-authoritative commands, assistance history, immutable evidence, and XP/mastery separation retain their existing gates.
- Human comprehension/enjoyment remains a separate checkpoint; neither screenshot inspection nor tests prove it.
